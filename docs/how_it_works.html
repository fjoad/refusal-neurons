Kazemi, Chegini & Safi (Apple), 2026 · arXiv 2605.08513

One number inside Qwen3-8B can switch off refusal

Out of 294,912 MLP neurons searched, one coordinate fires when a harmful request ends and the model is about to answer. Pin it the other way and the model stops refusing.

model Qwen3-8B neuron L14:F7924 pinned to +20 JailbreakBench ASR 95% (vs 5% unmodified, Llama Guard judge)
1 · Where it lives

Inside the MLP, not in the residual stream

Arditi et al. and our EMNLP paper work in the residual stream: the 4,096-number vector each token carries from layer to layer. There, refusal shows up as a direction, a weighted mix of all 4,096 coordinates. A single coordinate means nothing on its own, because nothing in the model treats the axes specially. Rotate them and every computation downstream is unchanged.

Each layer's MLP block expands that vector to 12,288 numbers and passes each one through its own nonlinearity. That one-coordinate-at-a-time step makes the axes special. This is what a privileged basis means: coordinate 7924 can learn a job of its own. The paper calls each of these coordinates a neuron.

Where the hook sits

The neuron is read at one exact spot: after the gate and value are multiplied, just before down_proj projects the 12,288 numbers back to 4,096 and adds them into the residual stream. Everything the paper measures and every intervention it makes happens on that one coordinate of h.

2 · What one neuron computes

Gate × value

Qwen3 and Llama-3.1 use a SwiGLU MLP. For one neuron i the value at the hook is

hi = SiLU(gi) × ui   where g = W_gate·x, u = W_up·x

The value u is what the neuron says, and it can have either sign. The gate SiLU(g) is how much of it gets through, like a tap on a pipe. SiLU is close to zero for negative g and close to g for positive g, so most of the time the gate is either shut or open and the value decides the sign of h.

We have not yet split h into gate and value for our three neurons. It costs one extra hook on W_gate, and it would tell us whether −4 at the turn boundary is an open gate on a negative value, which is the reading the paper assumes.

3 · Two poles and a sign

Which way the neuron fires depends on the model

A neuron can be strongly positive or strongly negative, and each side can mean something different. The paper calls the side a neuron takes on harmful prompts its harmful pole, and the opposite side its safe pole. They looked at the top-activating text on each side across 20K documents from The Pile:

  • The harmful pole mostly fires on explicit, age-restricted or legally regulated material.
  • The safe pole, when it has a coherent theme, fires on talk about restrictions: content warnings, disclaimers, privacy and regulatory notices. Qwen3-14B L17:F2154 is the clearest case. Qwen3-8B L14:F7924 has no coherent safe pole, so it behaves like a one-sided detector.

The sign itself is arbitrary. In Qwen3 1.7B–14B the harmful pole is negative; in Qwen3-32B and both Llama models it is positive. The intervention always pins the neuron opposite to its harmful pole, so +20 for Qwen3-8B and −4 for Llama-3.1-8B.

4 · Finding it

Score every neuron, then let attack success pick

For Qwen3-8B the search covers the first two thirds of the model: 24 layers × 12,288 neurons = 294,912 candidates. It needs one forward and one backward pass per training prompt.

  1. Run the training prompts

    128 harmful prompts (AdvBench, MaliciousInstruct, TDC2023) and 128 harmless ones (Alpaca), the Arditi et al. splits. Record h at the post-instruction tokens: the template tokens between the end of the user's message and the start of the answer.

  2. Measure how much each neuron pushes refusal

    Define p_refusal as the probability of the model's own refusal phrases (for Qwen3: “I'm sorry, but I can't help with that request.” and “I'm unable to assist”). The loss is the negative refusal log-odds, and its gradient with respect to each hi says which way moving that neuron moves refusal.

    L = −log( p_refusal / (1 − p_refusal) )

  3. Score = gradient × activation gap

    scorei,t = (ḡharmful + ḡharmless) × (āharmless − āharmful)

    Keep each neuron's best token t*. Drop any neuron that is not more active, in absolute size, on harmful prompts than on harmless ones.

  4. Rerank the top 5 by attack success

    For each of the 5 best-scoring neurons, sweep the pinned value m from 0 to 20 in steps of 2, then 20 to 200 in steps of 5 (47 values), in the direction opposite to the harmful pole. Measure ASR on 191 HarmBench prompts. The best (layer, neuron, m*) wins and goes to the JailbreakBench test set unchanged.

  5. Result for Qwen3-8B

    (14, 7924, +20)

Why the product finds gates

Take L14:F7924 at the newline. The gap is −0.23 − (−4.41) = +4.18. Pushing the neuron positive cuts refusal, so the loss goes up and the gradient is positive. The product is large and positive.

A neuron that separates the prompts but doesn't affect refusal has a gradient near zero. One that affects refusal but fires the same on both sets has a gap near zero. Only a neuron that does both scores high. For Llama, both signs flip and the product is still positive.

Our status (M2): the search is written and reviewed. The acceptance test is whether it puts L13:F3270 and L14:F7924 in its own top 5, and then picks them in the rerank.

5 · Switching it off

Constant pin or anchor

Constant

hi ← m*

The same value at every token, during the prompt and during generation. It works, but it forces the neuron far outside its normal range on every prompt, including harmless ones. Average cost across the seven models: −8.8 points MMLU, −1.2 GSM8K.

Anchor

hi ← clamp( k · m* · v / d − d, m* )

First a clean forward pass reads the prompt's own activation v: the minimum over the post-instruction tokens when d < 0, the maximum when d > 0. Prompts that fire like harmful ones get pushed to m*. Prompts near zero get only a small offset, about |d|. The clamp stops overshoot, and k ∈ {1, 2} is picked on HarmBench.

6 · What we ask on top

Is it the same neuron in every language and modality?

The paper is English-only and text-only. Our question is whether the gate is universal: when the harmful request arrives in Arabic, as an image, or as speech, does the same coordinate fire at the same turn boundary, and does pinning it still switch off refusal?

done

Weak test: does the English neuron fire in Arabic?

Yes, at the same token, and pinning it lowers refusal in both languages under a rough string-match check. It can't tell us whether Arabic has its own, stronger gate.

next

Strong test: run the search in Arabic

Score all 294,912 neurons on Arabic splits and rerank. If (14, 7924) comes out on top again, the gate is shared. If a different neuron wins, refusal is gated per language. This needs M2 to pass first, and one judge that reads both languages.

later

Images and speech

Use vision and audio models built on the same Qwen3 or Llama backbone, so the same (layer, neuron) exists. Then ask the same two questions when the request is an image of text or spoken audio.

later

Neurons, directions, SAEs

Our EMNLP paper found several category-specific refusal directions sharing a core. Working hypothesis: one gate neuron decides whether to refuse, and the category directions shape how.

Also in the paper
  • The neurons exist before alignment. In Qwen3-8B-Base the same L14:F7924 already separates harmful from harmless prompts, but it fires on the harmful content words rather than at the turn boundary. Alignment seems to rewire when it fires.
  • One neuron is a decent detector. On XSTest, Llama-3.1-8B L11:F4258 alone reaches AUROC 0.969, against 0.975 for Llama-Guard-3-8B.
  • Concept neurons work the other way. Adding to a single “suicide” neuron makes the model bring the topic into innocent prompts. Refusal neurons gate whether harmful knowledge comes out; concept neurons hold the knowledge.
